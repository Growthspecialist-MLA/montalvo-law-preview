Can a Foreclosure Be Wrong? Standing and Procedural Defenses in Florida

Published August 7, 2026

Florida is a judicial foreclosure state, which means a lender can't just repossess a home. Every foreclosure is a lawsuit, filed in circuit court, and the lender has to prove its case the same way any plaintiff does. That requirement sounds obvious, but it's the exact thing that trips up a surprising number of foreclosure filings, and it's where a case genuinely gets won or lost, long before anyone talks about missed payments.

The lender has to prove it actually owns your note

To foreclose, a lender has to establish standing, meaning it has to prove it held or owned the promissory note at the time the lawsuit was filed, not afterward. That timing detail matters more than it sounds like it should. In McLean v. JP Morgan Chase Bank Nat'l Ass'n, Florida's Fourth District Court of Appeal reversed a foreclosure judgment because the bank's own assignment of the mortgage was dated three days after it filed the lawsuit. The bank couldn't prove it owned the loan on the date that actually counted.

Mortgages get sold, bundled into trusts, and transferred between servicers constantly. Every one of those transfers is supposed to be documented. In BAC Funding Consortium, Inc. v. Jean-Jacques, Florida's Second District Court of Appeal held that an incomplete, unsigned, and unauthenticated assignment isn't good enough to prove standing. If there's a gap in that paper trail, or the document a bank relies on doesn't actually say what it needs to say, that's not a technicality. It's a real basis to challenge the entire case.

Federal rules on how a servicer has to handle your file

Separate from who owns the note, federal law controls how a mortgage servicer has to treat you while you're trying to work something out. Under Regulation X, 12 C.F.R. § 1024.41, a servicer generally can't move for a foreclosure judgment or a sale while a complete loss mitigation application, like a modification request, is still pending review, if that application came in more than 37 days before the scheduled sale. Pursuing both paths at once anyway is commonly called dual tracking, and it's a specific, enforceable violation, not just an unfair-feeling business practice.

What this looks like in a real case

These defenses aren't abstract. They show up as concrete questions asked of the actual paperwork in your file:

  • Does the assignment of mortgage attached to the complaint have a date that's before, or after, the lawsuit was actually filed?
  • Is the affidavit used to prove the amount owed signed by someone who can show they personally reviewed your account, or does it read like a template signed in bulk?
  • If you submitted a complete modification application, did the servicer keep moving the foreclosure forward anyway, without deciding on that application first?

Once served with a foreclosure complaint, Florida gives you roughly 20 days to file a formal response. That deadline is what makes these defenses usable in the first place. Raised early, in the answer, they can stop a case from ever reaching a default or a swift summary judgment. Discovered later, after judgment, they're far harder to undo. If you've been served, get the paperwork reviewed immediately, not after you've decided whether the case feels worth fighting.

The takeaway

A foreclosure being filed doesn't mean it's automatically valid. Whether the lender can actually prove ownership of your note, and whether it followed federal servicing rules while doing it, are real, specific questions with real answers in your file. A free consultation starts with pulling that file apart to see what it actually shows.
