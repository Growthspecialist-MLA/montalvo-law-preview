Deficiency Judgments in Florida After Foreclosure — What Changed in 2026

Published August 7, 2026

Florida had the highest foreclosure rate in the country heading into 2026. If you're one of the homeowners behind on payments right now, there's a real chance you've already thought through what happens to your credit if the house is sold in foreclosure. Fewer people have thought through what happens to the debt itself, specifically, whether your lender can come after you personally for the difference between what you owed and what the house actually sold for. That's a deficiency judgment, and as of this year, there's a second problem stacked on top of it that didn't exist twelve months ago.

What a deficiency judgment actually is

When a lender forecloses and the home sells for less than the remaining loan balance, the gap between the two is the deficiency. In Florida, a lender isn't automatically entitled to collect it. They have to pursue a deficiency judgment, either as part of the foreclosure case itself or in a separate lawsuit, and Florida law puts real limits on what they can get:

  • If the property is owner-occupied and residential, the deficiency can't exceed the difference between what's owed and the home's fair market value on the date of the sale, not just the sale price itself, which matters if the property sold at a depressed foreclosure-auction price.
  • If your home had a homestead exemption on file with the county before the foreclosure was filed, there's a rebuttable presumption that it qualifies as owner-occupied, which shifts the deficiency cap in your favor unless the lender proves otherwise.
  • The lender has one year from the foreclosure sale to seek a deficiency judgment. Miss that window, and the claim is gone.
  • If a court already denied a deficiency claim inside the foreclosure case itself, the lender can't come back later and try again in a separate lawsuit.

None of this happens automatically in your favor. These are protections that have to be raised and argued, usually while the foreclosure case is still active, not discovered afterward when a collection notice shows up.

The part most people don't see coming: the tax bill

Here's what changed. For years, homeowners who negotiated a short sale or a deed-in-lieu of foreclosure, or had a portion of their mortgage debt forgiven, could exclude that forgiven amount from taxable income under the Qualified Principal Residence Indebtedness exclusion. The IRS confirms that exclusion applied to debt forgiven in tax years 2021 through 2025. It expired January 1, 2026, and as of this writing, Congress has not passed a further extension — a renewal bill was introduced in 2025, but it isn't law.

What that means in plain terms: if your lender forgives $40,000 of mortgage debt as part of a short sale or deed-in-lieu closed this year, the IRS generally treats that $40,000 as ordinary income unless a different exception applies. The two most common exceptions still standing are the insolvency exclusion (if your total debts exceeded your total assets immediately before the cancellation) and the rule that non-recourse loans don't create cancellation-of-debt income in the first place. Both require a tax professional to evaluate against your actual numbers. This isn't something to assume your way through.

The practical effect is that a short sale or deed-in-lieu negotiated in 2026 can cost more, in real after-tax dollars, than the identical deal would have cost a year ago. That's not a reason to avoid those options. It's a reason to have the deficiency waiver and the tax exposure both on the table at the same time you're negotiating, not treated as two separate problems that show up months apart.

Where this actually gets handled

A deficiency judgment isn't something you wait to respond to after it's filed. The leverage is earlier than that:

  • Negotiating an explicit deficiency waiver into the short sale or deed-in-lieu agreement itself, so the lender contractually gives up the right to come after you for the difference, rather than leaving it ambiguous.
  • Challenging the underlying foreclosure, since a case that ends in your favor, or that settles on better terms, changes what deficiency exposure even exists.
  • Coordinating with a tax professional on the insolvency exclusion before the cancellation happens, not after the 1099-C shows up in your mailbox the following January.

If you're weighing a short sale, a deed-in-lieu, or you've already gotten a notice that a deficiency judgment is being pursued against you, talk to the firm before you sign anything. The waiver language in these agreements is negotiated once, and it's much harder to fix after the fact than to get right the first time.

The bottom line for 2026

Florida's deficiency judgment rules haven't changed this year. What changed is the tax treatment sitting on top of them. Anyone negotiating a way out of foreclosure right now needs both pieces looked at together: what the lender can still come after you for, and what the IRS will treat as income when they let some of it go.
