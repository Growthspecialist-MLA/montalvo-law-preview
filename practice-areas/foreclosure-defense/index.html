Foreclosure Defense

Florida Foreclosure Defense

A foreclosure complaint begins a legal process. It does not by itself establish that the plaintiff is entitled to a foreclosure judgment. The creditor generally must establish the existence of the obligation, its right to enforce it, the borrower's default, compliance with applicable contractual and legal requirements, and the amount due.

Where It Starts

Examining the Foreclosure Claim

Montalvo Law Firm examines the foreclosure claim by reviewing the documents and factual history underlying those elements.

Depending upon the case, foreclosure analysis may include:

  • the note, mortgage, HELOC agreement, or other loan documents
  • assignments and transfers
  • the identity of the party claiming enforcement rights
  • standing when the foreclosure was filed
  • lost-note allegations
  • endorsements and allonges where legally relevant
  • prior foreclosure proceedings
  • loan modifications
  • payment histories
  • escrow records
  • payment-change notices
  • default and acceleration notices
  • payoff and reinstatement statements
  • servicing transfers
  • fees and advances
  • the evidence available to prove the amount claimed

A defense that matters in one foreclosure may have little relevance in another. The analysis must begin with the actual loan and its history.

Standing

Standing and the Right to Enforce

Foreclosure requires more than showing that a mortgage exists.

The party seeking foreclosure must establish the legal right to enforce the underlying obligation and mortgage. The precise requirements depend upon the nature of the instrument and applicable law.

Questions may arise concerning possession, endorsements, assignments, ownership, transfer of enforcement rights, lost notes, or whether the plaintiff possessed the required rights at the legally relevant time.

Lost Notes

When the Original Note Is Missing

A foreclosure plaintiff that cannot produce the original note may attempt to enforce a lost instrument.

Lost-note cases can require careful examination of:

  • who previously possessed the original
  • when and how possession was lost
  • whether the plaintiff acquired enforceable rights
  • the history of prior litigation involving the original instrument
  • transfers occurring before and after the loss
  • whether the statutory requirements for enforcement of a lost instrument have been satisfied

A mortgage assignment does not automatically answer every question concerning enforcement of the underlying debt.

Servicing

Servicing-Created Defaults

Sometimes the central issue is not simply whether the homeowner stopped making payments.

A disputed payment increase, escrow adjustment, servicing transfer, misapplication of payments, or other account error can potentially affect the payment demanded and the servicer's determination that the loan is in default.

In those cases, the servicing history may need to be reconstructed to determine:

  • What payment was contractually required?
  • Why did the amount change?
  • Were payments properly applied?
  • Did a servicing event contribute to the alleged delinquency?
  • Is the foreclosure based upon an accurate account history?

Consumer Protection

Mortgage-Related Consumer Protection and Counterclaims

Depending on the facts of a particular case, the type of loan, and the identity and conduct of the creditor, servicer, debt buyer, or collector involved, the firm's analysis may also include potential claims or counterclaims under consumer-protection laws such as the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA), the Florida Consumer Collection Practices Act (FCCPA), the Real Estate Settlement Procedures Act (RESPA) and applicable mortgage-servicing regulations, and the Truth in Lending Act (TILA) and Regulation Z.

Potential issues may include, depending on the circumstances:

  • materially false or misleading statements concerning the amount, character, or legal status of a mortgage debt
  • attempts to collect interest, fees, advances, escrow amounts, or other charges not authorized by the loan agreement or applicable law
  • inaccurate default, reinstatement, or payoff information
  • servicing errors or improper servicing conduct that contributed to an asserted default or foreclosure
  • misleading representations concerning ownership or enforcement rights
  • failure to provide or properly respond to information or servicing-error requests where applicable
  • other collection or servicing conduct prohibited by federal or Florida law

Not every foreclosure, servicing dispute, or collection communication creates a violation. Whether a particular statute applies depends upon the loan, the parties involved, the timing, and the specific conduct.
