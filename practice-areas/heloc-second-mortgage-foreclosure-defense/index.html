HELOC & Second-Mortgage Foreclosure Defense

HELOC and Second-Mortgage Foreclosures Require Their Own Analysis

A home-equity line of credit is not necessarily the same type of legal obligation as a conventional fixed-sum mortgage note. A HELOC generally permits advances during a draw period up to an agreed credit limit, and because of that structure, the legal rules governing enforcement may differ from those governing a traditional negotiable promissory note.

Classification

Is the HELOC a Negotiable Instrument?

One of the first questions may be the legal classification of the HELOC agreement.

If the obligation is non-negotiable, concepts associated with Article 3 of the Uniform Commercial Code, such as holder status, negotiation by endorsement, or an allonge, may not determine who owns or may enforce the debt. Instead, the analysis may require examination of contractual ownership and transfer principles, Article 9, assignments, account-level transfers, and the history through which the present creditor claims its rights.

The result depends upon the actual loan documents and governing law.

Ownership History

Tracing Ownership and Enforcement Rights

A second-mortgage or HELOC foreclosure may involve several different entities over the life of the loan. The history can include:

  • the originating lender
  • subsequent owners
  • securitization or trust transfers
  • debt purchasers
  • servicers and subservicers
  • assignments of mortgage
  • separate transfers of the underlying obligation

The key question is not simply whether the current plaintiff's name appears on a recent assignment.

The relevant inquiry may require determining how the underlying contractual rights traveled from the original lender to the party now seeking foreclosure and whether the evidence establishes each necessary transfer.

Assignments

Mortgage Assignments and the Underlying Debt

The history of the mortgage and the history of the underlying debt should be examined together.

A recorded mortgage assignment may be important, but it does not necessarily resolve every question concerning ownership or enforcement of the underlying obligation.

Where different documents reflect different transfer paths, the records may need to be reconciled before the plaintiff's asserted enforcement rights can be evaluated.

Dormant Debt

Dormant or "Zombie" Second Mortgages

Some second mortgages and HELOCs remained inactive for many years following the financial crisis.

A homeowner may have received few or no communications concerning the junior loan and then, years later, receive a substantial collection demand or foreclosure complaint. These cases may require examination of:

  • the account's servicing history
  • whether the loan was charged off
  • the communications sent to the borrower
  • accumulated interest and fees
  • ownership and servicing transfers
  • limitations issues
  • loss-mitigation history
  • the basis for the amount now demanded

The age of the loan alone does not determine whether foreclosure is barred. The entire history must be analyzed.

Accounting

Is the Amount Claimed Correct?

HELOC cases often require careful accounting because the credit limit is not the same thing as the amount owed. The analysis may include:

  • advances
  • payments
  • interest
  • fees
  • escrow charges
  • servicing advances
  • taxes
  • insurance
  • payoff calculations
  • unexplained account entries

A creditor seeking foreclosure should be able to explain how the amount demanded was calculated and identify the contractual or legal basis for material charges.

Consumer Protection

Mortgage-Related Consumer Protection and Counterclaims

The firm's analysis is not limited to whether the plaintiff can prove the foreclosure claim. Depending upon the facts, the type of loan, the parties involved, and the conduct at issue, conduct by a creditor, servicer, debt buyer, foreclosure law firm, or other qualifying debt collector may itself give rise to affirmative claims or counterclaims under the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA), the Florida Consumer Collection Practices Act (FCCPA), the Real Estate Settlement Procedures Act (RESPA) and applicable Regulation X servicing requirements, the Truth in Lending Act (TILA) and Regulation Z, and other applicable federal or Florida consumer-protection statutes and regulations.

This conduct may be examined both before a foreclosure action is filed and during the litigation itself, where applicable. Examples may include:

  • false or misleading statements concerning the amount, character, or legal status of the debt
  • demands for unauthorized interest, fees, advances, escrow amounts, or other charges
  • inaccurate payment-change, default, reinstatement, or payoff information
  • servicing errors that contributed to or created an asserted default
  • misleading representations concerning ownership, standing, or enforcement rights
  • failures to properly respond to servicing information requests or asserted errors, where applicable

Not every foreclosure, servicing dispute, or collection communication creates an actionable claim. Statutory coverage, limitations periods, damages, causation, and the identity of the proper defendant must be evaluated on a case-by-case basis.

Forum

State Court or Bankruptcy?

In appropriate cases, HELOC and junior-mortgage disputes may also have bankruptcy implications.

The classification of the obligation, validity and amount of the creditor's claim, property equity, lien position, and the homeowner's broader financial circumstances may affect whether certain issues can or should be addressed in state foreclosure court, bankruptcy court, or both.
